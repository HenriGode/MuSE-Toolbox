================================================================================
SCIENTIFIC WRITING STYLE & TERMINOLOGY GUIDELINES
Acoustic Signal Processing & Machine Learning (Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg)
================================================================================

1. CORE PHILOSOPHY: UNCOMPROMISING CONSISTENCY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Never vary terminology for the sake of stylistic variety. If the same entity,
  concept, parameter, or hardware component is meant, ALWAYS use the EXACT same term.
- Synonyms create ambiguity in scientific writing. Consistency is not considered
  repetitive or boring; it is essential for precision, clarity, and preventing
  any misunderstanding by reviewers and readers.

2. PREFERRED TERMINOLOGY & CANONICAL NAMING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Hardware & Acoustic Sensors:
  * ALWAYS use "microphone", "microphones", "microphone array", "microphone pairs",
    and "microphone count" (M).
  * NEVER use "sensor", "sensors", "sensor array", or "sensor count" unless explicitly
    referring to generalized non-acoustic sensor networks.

- Sources, Scene Entities & Counting: Sound Sources vs. Speakers:
  * Theoretical & Methodological Sections (Signal Model, Problem Formulation, Feature Extraction, Architecture):
    - The physics, signal formulation, spatial statistics, spatial features (IPD, PSC, GMSC, wGMSC),
      and neural network architecture are designed for GENERAL acoustic sound fields and should
      generalize to any acoustic source type.
    - Therefore, in these sections (Sections I, II, III, IV), ALWAYS strictly use:
      "sound source(s)", "active sound source(s)", "concurrent sound sources", and "source count" (K_t).
    - NEVER use "speaker", "talker", or "speaker count" in these general theoretical/methodological sections.
  * Dataset & Experimental Sections (Datasets, Experimental Setup, Evaluation Metrics, Results):
    - In the entire Datasets section and speech-based experimental evaluation (using LibriSpeech,
      BRUDEX, AMI meeting corpus, CHiME-6), all evaluated scenarios exclusively comprise human speech utterances.
    - Therefore, in these empirical sections, ALWAYS consistently use:
      "speaker(s)", "active speaker(s)", "concurrent speakers", "single-speaker",
      "conversational partners", and "speaker count".
    - Avoid using "sound source" when referring to the human participants, their physical positions,
      or their relative powers in the dataset descriptions (e.g., use "active speakers",
      "the speakers maintain a minimum distance", "relative speaker powers").
    - Exception: Standard acoustic simulation methodology terms retain their canonical names
      (e.g., "image source method", "clean speech signal components").

- Acoustic Transfer Functions & Directional Quantities:
  * ALWAYS use "acoustic transfer function (ATF)" and "ATF vector" (a_k).
  * NEVER use "spatial steering vector", "steering vector", or "steering factor".

- Time & Frequency Units:
  * Time: ALWAYS use "time frame t" (or "STFT time frame t") for discrete frame indices,
    and "discrete-time sample index l" for sample-level indices.
  * Frequency: ALWAYS use "frequency bin f" (or "STFT frequency bin f").
  * Lengths & Shifts: Use "window length L_win" and "frame shift L_hop".

- Smoothing Parameters & Physical Constants:
  * Time Constants: Always explicitly specify the time smoothing constant tau_alpha
    together with the STFT frame shift tau_fs = L_hop / f_s, and explicitly give the
    relation for the exponential forgetting factor: alpha = exp(-tau_fs / tau_alpha).
  * Never introduce a forgetting factor alpha without connecting it to its time constant tau_alpha.

- Causality & Latency:
  * ALWAYS use "strictly causal" (with explicit constraint tau <= t, zero look-ahead).
  * NEVER use "causal" ambiguously without defining whether future look-ahead is permitted.

- Hyphenation Standards (Standardize across the entire paper):
  * "multi-channel" (always hyphenated; never "multichannel")
  * "multi-microphone" (always hyphenated; never "multimicrophone")
  * "time-frequency" (always hyphenated; never "time frequency" or "T-F")
  * "real-valued" (always hyphenated)
  * "complex-valued" (always hyphenated)
  * "look-ahead" (always hyphenated; never "lookahead")
  * "frame-level" / "frame-wise" (always hyphenated)

3. MATHEMATICAL & LOGICAL RIGOR: DEFINE ESSENTIALS, AVOID PEDANTRY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Core Tenet: Perfect exactness and pure logic. Never state anything false, vague,
  or mathematically ungrounded.
- Define Domain-Specific Symbols & Spaces:
  * Every specialized variable, parameter, set, indexing range, and domain-specific
    operator must be clearly defined before (or immediately upon) its introduction.
  * State the mathematical space for core quantities:
    - Scalar: e.g., s_{k,t} \in \mathbb{C}, K_t \in \{0, 1, \dots, K_{\max}\}
    - Vector: e.g., \mathbf{y}_t \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times 1}, \mathbf{z}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{2F \times 1}
    - Matrix: e.g., \mathbf{R}_{y,t} \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times M}, \mathbf{A}_t \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times K_t}
    - Tensor: e.g., \mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D \times T}
  * Standard operations like the imaginary unit \mathrm{i} = \sqrt{-1}, conjugate
    transpose \{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{H}}, transpose \{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{T}}, expectation \mathbb{E}\{\cdot\},
    and matrix rank \mathrm{rank}\{\cdot\} should be explicitly identified on first appearance.
- Avoid Pedantic Over-Defining:
  * Do NOT patronize the reader by stating obvious value ranges for every scalar parameter.
  * Assume the reader is a mathematically literate researcher who understands standard
    composite notation (e.g., the matrix exponent -\mathrm{H}/2, standard exponential decay,
    and elementary trigonometric identities).
  * Explicitly note "element-wise" application when applying scalar functions (log, cos, sin)
    to vectors, and define matrix indexing notation [\mathbf{A}]_{i,j} on first appearance.

4. COMPLETENESS, CONCISENESS & SMART REUSE ("EINDEUTIG" & STRAIGHT TO THE POINT)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Completeness ("Eindeutig"):
  * Methodological and algorithmic descriptions must be fully specified down to the
    necessary operational details.
  * Leave zero gaps, ambiguities, or unanswered questions about what operations are performed,
    how inputs are processed, or what dimensions result. Everything must be deterministic
    and unambiguous.
- Maximum Conciseness through Directness:
  * Deliver explanations straight to the point. Write compactly without conversational
    windups, filler words, or narrative detours.
- Absolute Ban on Redundancy:
  * NEVER define, explain, or motivate the exact same concept twice.
  * If a concept, physical intuition, or parameter has already been introduced, do NOT
    repeat its explanation in later paragraphs.
- Smart Reuse via References (\Cref):
  * When relying on an earlier model, equation, or parameter, point back directly
    using \Cref{eq:...} or \Cref{sec:...} rather than re-explaining it in prose.
  * Exception: If the original definition appeared much earlier in a distant section,
    a single, very brief reminder clause is permissible to maintain reading flow,
    but never a full re-derivation or repetition.

5. OBJECTIVE DESCRIPTION VS. SELECTIVE MOTIVATION (THE "WHAT" VS. THE "WHY")
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Primary Focus: Objective Description of "What Was Done":
  * The main body of technical sections should objectively, clearly, and precisely describe
    what was done so that any peer can reproduce the work.
- Do NOT Over-Motivate Every Microscopic Detail:
  * Avoid arguing, philosophizing, or justifying routine engineering choices and feature
    representations (e.g., do not write lengthy defenses of why polar vs. Cartesian
    coordinates were chosen for a feature vector).
- Reserve the "Why" for the Big Picture & Core Hypotheses:
  * Restrict explicit motivation to the overarching scientific narrative, central hypotheses,
    and core physical principles (e.g., why spatial coherence relates to source count,
    why temporal whitening reveals newly activating sources in a mixture).
  * Motivate choices that will actually be substantiated, verified, and discussed in the
    evaluation and experimental results.

6. PUNCTUATION & SYNTAX RULES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- STRICT BAN ON EM-DASHES:
  * NEVER use em-dashes ("---") in text.
  * Separate parenthetical or qualifying clauses naturally using commas, parentheses,
    or distinct sentences.
- Active & Precise Academic Style:
  * Write in clear, direct, and grammatically complete sentences.
  * Avoid colloquial phrasing, vague qualifiers ("somehow", "more or less"),
    and excessive introductory fluff.

7. MATHEMATICAL NOTATION & OPERATOR CONVENTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Operator Bracing:
  * Functional operators and matrix transformations ALWAYS take curly braces:
    \bm{\Gamma}\{\cdot\}, \mathrm{diag}\{\cdot\}, \operatorname{gmsc}\{\cdot\},
    \lambda_{\max}\{\cdot\}, \mathrm{rank}\{\cdot\}, \Re\{\cdot\}, \Im\{\cdot\},
    \mathbb{E}\{\cdot\}, \log\{\cdot\}.
- Matrix Square Roots:
  * Explicitly define matrix square roots upon first use:
    \mathbf{R}^{\nicefrac{\mathrm{H}}{2}} \mathbf{R}^{\nicefrac{1}{2}} = \mathbf{R} (e.g., Cholesky).
- Unified Tensor Convention:
  * All feature tensors follow the unified shape: C \times D \times T
    where C = spatial channel or microphone pair dimension,
          D = feature dimension,
          T = sequence dimension (STFT time frames).

8. LATEX FORMATTING & CROSS-REFERENCING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Mandatory Textual Referencing of All Visual & Structural Elements:
  * EVERY table, figure, flowchart, diagram, algorithm, or structural overview
    MUST be explicitly referenced and introduced in the running body text using \Cref{...}
    (e.g., \Cref{tab:...}, \Cref{fig:...}, \Cref{alg:...}).
  * Never leave "orphan" floats that exist without being referenced and contextualized
    in the text.
  * The reference must smoothly integrate into the surrounding discussion, pointing the
    reader to the element and explaining its core purpose and takeaway.
- Cross-Referencing via cleveref:
  * ALWAYS use cleveref (\Cref{...}) for all internal cross-references:
    - \Cref{sec:...} for sections and subsections
    - \Cref{eq:...} for equations (renders automatically as (1), (2) in IEEE style)
    - \Cref{tab:...} for tables
    - \Cref{fig:...} for figures and flowcharts
    - \Cref{alg:...} for algorithms and pseudocode
- Tables:
  * Use booktabs (\toprule, \midrule, \bottomrule) without vertical lines.
  * Table descriptions and table cells must follow a consistent capitalization scheme
    (capitalize the first letter of descriptive entries).
- Acronyms:
  * Register all technical abbreviations in the glossaries package (\newacronym).
  * Use \gls{...} and \glspl{...} for automated expansion on first use.

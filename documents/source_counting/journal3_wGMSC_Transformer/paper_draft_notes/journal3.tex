\begin{abstract}
  Accurately estimating the number of active sound sources in real-time is a fundamental prerequisite for multi-microphone acoustic signal processing tasks, such as source separation, localization, and assistive listening. Existing deep neural network (DNN)-based source counting methods often rely on long observation windows that introduce unacceptable latency for online applications, or they are strictly constrained to fixed microphone array geometries. In this paper, we propose a strictly STFT-frame causal, online acoustic sound source counting system that handles a variable number of input channels and dynamically adapts to arbitrary array geometries. Our approach leverages a causal Transformer-based architecture combined with robust spatial features, specifically the Whitened Generalized Magnitude Squared Coherence (GMSC). By employing time-reversed whitening, the network tracks both source activations and deactivations by measuring spatial coherence. Unlike traditional overlapped speech detection systems that rely on linguistic alignment, our model is trained on a purely acoustic power-based ground truth, allowing it to generalize to diverse, non-speech acoustic sources. To achieve array geometry agnosticism, the system utilizes an early attention-pooling mechanism to unify variable microphone counts, alongside a novel late-fusion strategy that injects physical microphone coordinates directly into the network via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers. We comprehensively evaluate the proposed geometry-conditioned system through zero-shot testing on both simulated environments and real-world datasets, including BRUDEX, CHiME-6, and AMI. Our results demonstrate the effectiveness of combining strictly causal Transformers with physical coordinate conditioning to achieve robust, real-time sound source counting across mismatched and dynamic array configurations. We comprehensively evaluate the proposed geometry-conditioned system through zero-shot testing on both simulated environments and real-world datasets, including BRUDEX, CHiME-6, and AMI. Our results demonstrate the effectiveness of combining strictly causal Transformers with physical coordinate conditioning to achieve robust, real-time sound source counting across mismatched and dynamic array configurations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Signal Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:signal_model}

In this section, we formulate the acoustic signal model in the time and \gls{STFT} domains, describe the underlying second-order spatial statistics with \gls{ATF} vectors, and formally define the strictly causal frame-level sound source counting problem.

\subsection{Acoustic Signal Model in the Time and STFT Domains}
\label{sec:signal_model_stft}
We consider an acoustic scenario with $K_{\mathrm{max}}$ sound sources captured by an arbitrary array of $M$ microphones. Let $y_{m,l}$ denote the discrete-time signal recorded at the $m$-th microphone ($m \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$), sampled at a sampling frequency $f_s$, where $l \in \{1, \ldots, L\}$ denotes the discrete-time sample index and $L$ is the total number of samples in the considered signal segment.

By applying the \gls{STFT} with an analysis window of length $L_{\mathrm{win}}$ and a frame shift of $L_{\mathrm{hop}}$ samples, the microphone signals are transformed into the time-frequency domain. The total number of \gls{STFT} frames $T$ obtained from a segment of $L$ samples is given by
\begin{align}
  T = \left\lfloor \frac{L - L_{\mathrm{win}}}{L_{\mathrm{hop}}} \right\rfloor + 1.
  \label{eq:num_frames_def}
\end{align}
Discarding the non-informative DC ($f=0$) and Nyquist ($f=L_{\mathrm{win}}/2$) components yields $F = L_{\mathrm{win}}/2 - 1$ frequency bins. Stacking the \gls{STFT} coefficients of all $M$ microphones yields the multi-channel observation vector:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{y}_{t,f} = \begin{bmatrix} y_{1,t,f} & y_{2,t,f} & \cdots & y_{M,t,f} \end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}} \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times 1},
  \label{eq:sig_vec_def}
\end{align}
where $t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}$ and $f \in \{1, \ldots, F\}$ denote the time frame index and frequency bin index, respectively, and $\{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{T}}$ denotes the transpose operator. For notational conciseness, the frequency bin index $f$ is omitted in the remainder of this section (i.e., $\mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{y}_{t,f}$) whenever narrowband operations are implied.

At each time frame $t$, the multi-microphone signal $\mathbf{y}_t$ is composed of the superposition of the acoustic contributions from all currently active sound sources and an additive ambient noise component $\mathbf{n}_t \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}$, i.e.,
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{y}_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} \mathbf{x}_{k,t} + \mathbf{n}_t,
  \label{eq:acoustic_mixture_model}
\end{align}
where $K_t \in \{0, 1, \ldots, K_{\mathrm{max}}\}$ denotes the instantaneous number of active sound sources at time frame $t$. Importantly, $K_t$ is a broadband quantity (i.e., source activity is assumed to be frequency-independent across all frequency bins), and $\mathbf{x}_{k,t} \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}$ denotes the spatial component of the $k$-th sound source.

Under the \gls{MTF} approximation~\cite{avargel_multiplicative_2007}, the spatial source component vector $\mathbf{x}_{k,t}$ can be expressed as the product of the emitted source signal $s_{k,t} \in \mathbb{C}$ and the (time-invariant) \gls{ATF} vector $\mathbf{a}_k \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}$, i.e.,
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{x}_{k,t} = \mathbf{a}_k s_{k,t} \quad \forall k \in \{1, \ldots, K_t\},
  \label{eq:atf_source_component}
\end{align}
with the \gls{ATF} vector of the $k$-th sound source defined as
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{a}_k = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1,k} & a_{2,k} & \cdots & a_{M,k} \end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}} \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times 1},
\end{align}
where $a_{m,k}$ represents the acoustic transfer function relating source $k$ to the $m$-th microphone. We assume that the \gls{ATF} vectors of all sources are linearly independent.
By collecting the \gls{ATF} vectors of the $K_t$ active sources into the mixing matrix
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{A}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \mathbf{a}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_{K_t} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times K_t},
\end{align}
and defining the source signal vector $\mathbf{s}_t = \begin{bmatrix} s_{1,t} & \cdots & s_{K_t,t} \end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}} \in \mathbb{C}^{K_t \times 1}$, the multi-microphone observation vector in~\Cref{eq:acoustic_mixture_model} can be compactly written as:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{y}_t = \mathbf{A}_t \mathbf{s}_t + \mathbf{n}_t.
  \label{eq:matrix_mixture_model}
\end{align}

\subsection{Second-Order Spatial Statistics}
\label{sec:second_order_statistics}
Assuming that the individual sound sources are mutually uncorrelated and uncorrelated with the additive ambient noise, the spatial covariance matrix $\mathbf{R}_{y,t} \in \mathbb{C}^{M\times M}$ of the noisy microphone signals at time frame $t$ is given by
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{R}_{y,t} = \mathbb{E}\left\{ \mathbf{y}_t \mathbf{y}_t^{\mathrm{H}} \right\} = \mathbf{R}_{x,t} + \mathbf{R}_{n,t},
  \label{eq:cov_model}
\end{align}
where $\mathbb{E}\{\cdot\}$ denotes the expectation operator, $\{\cdot\}^{\mathrm{H}}$ denotes the conjugate transpose operator, $\mathbf{R}_{n,t} = \mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{n}_t \mathbf{n}_t^{\mathrm{H}}\}$ denotes the noise covariance matrix, and $\mathbf{R}_{x,t} = \mathbb{E}\{\mathbf{x}_t \mathbf{x}_t^{\mathrm{H}}\}$ denotes the spatial covariance matrix of the sound sources, with $\mathbf{x}_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K_t}\mathbf{x}_{k,t}$. Substituting~\Cref{eq:matrix_mixture_model} into~\Cref{eq:cov_model} yields:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{R}_{x,t} = \mathbf{A}_t \bm{\Phi}_{s,t} \mathbf{A}_t^{\mathrm{H}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K_t} \phi_{s,k,t} \, \mathbf{a}_k \mathbf{a}_k^{\mathrm{H}},
  \label{eq:clean_cov_model}
\end{align}
where $\bm{\Phi}_{s,t} = \mathrm{diag}\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} \phi_{s,1,t} & \cdots & \phi_{s,K_t,t} \end{bmatrix} \right\} \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{K_t \times K_t}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the time-varying power spectral densities $\phi_{s,k,t} = \mathbb{E}\{|s_{k,t}|^2\} > 0$ of the actively contributing sound sources.

Because the \gls{ATF} vectors $\mathbf{a}_k$ are assumed to be linearly independent and the active source powers are strictly non-zero, the rank of the clean spatial covariance matrix $\mathbf{R}_{x,t}$ in~\Cref{eq:clean_cov_model} is directly determined by the number of active sources, i.e.,
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{rank}\left( \mathbf{R}_{x,t} \right) = \min(M, K_t).
  \label{eq:rank_relation}
\end{align}
This fundamental algebraic relationship between source multiplicity and spatial covariance matrix rank provides the theoretical foundation for multi-channel spatial analysis and spatial coherence metrics.

\subsection{Acoustic Sound Source Counting Framework}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
The core objective of this work is to estimate the instantaneous number of active sound sources $K_t$ at each time frame $t$ from the multi-channel acoustic observations. Formally, we define the sound source counting estimator $\mathcal{M}$ as a mapping from the multi-channel time-frequency observations to the estimated instantaneous source count $\widehat{K}_t$:
\begin{align}
  \widehat{K}_t = \mathcal{M}\left( \left\{ \mathbf{y}_{\tau, f} \right\}_{\tau=1, f=1}^{t, F} \right) \quad \forall t \in \{1, \ldots, T\}.
  \label{eq:strict_causality_definition}
\end{align}
To enable low-latency operation in real-time acoustic applications, we impose a strict \gls{STFT} frame-level causality constraint: the estimation at time frame $t$ is conditioned exclusively on current and past observations up to frame $t$ ($\tau \leq t$), with zero look-ahead into future frames ($\tau > t$). All neural network architectures evaluated in this work are designed to strictly comply with~\Cref{eq:strict_causality_definition}.

We formulate source counting as an integer classification problem with $(K_{\mathrm{max}} + 1)$ mutually exclusive classes, where $K_t = 0$ corresponds to source absence (ambient noise only). For a given audio segment comprising $T$ time frames, the ground-truth source count sequence is represented as a $T$-dimensional target vector:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{k} = \begin{bmatrix} K_1 & K_2 & \cdots & K_T \end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}} \in \{0, 1, \ldots, K_{\mathrm{max}}\}^{T}.
  \label{eq:target_vector}
\end{align}

It is essential to distinguish the acoustic ground truth used in this paper from conventional text-driven linguistic alignments commonly found in the speech diarization literature. Traditional diarization and voice activity detection benchmarks often define ground-truth activity using linguistic forced-alignment tools (e.g., the Montreal Forced Aligner~\cite{mcauliffe2017montreal}), which label speech activity strictly based on phonetically transcribed text. Consequently, non-linguistic vocalizations (such as coughs, throat clearings, laughter, and heavy breathing) as well as non-speech acoustic sound events are labeled as inactive, even though substantial directional acoustic power impinges upon the microphone array. In contrast, in this work we define $K_t$ based on \emph{acoustic sound presence}: any sound source producing energy above an acoustic presence threshold relative to the background noise is classified as active, ensuring the model reflects the physical reality of the sound field.

\subsection{Synthetic Dataset}

To provide sufficient training data and to analyze the impact of array geometry and channel count on the performance of the models,
randomized synthetic datasets are constructed.
The acoustic environments are simulated using the image source method
provided by the Pyroomacoustics library~\cite{scheibler2018pyroomacoustics} to generate \glspl{RIR}.
Shoebox rooms are generated with lengths and widths ranging from \SI{4.0}{\meter} to \SI{8.0}{\meter}
and heights between \SI{2.5}{\meter} and \SI{4.0}{\meter}.
The reverberation time ($RT_{60}$) is uniformly sampled between \SI{0.2}{\second} and \SI{0.8}{\second}.
To simulate realistic ambient background noise, diffuse noise is generated using the
ANF-Generator~\cite{habets_ANFgenerator_2021}\footnote{Code: \url{https://github.com/audiolabs/anf-generator}}
by spatializing single-channel noise recordings from the DEMAND database~\cite{thiemann2013demand}.
The DEMAND noise environments are partitioned by their acoustic categories:
Domestic, Nature, Office, and Public environments for training,
Transportation environments for validation,
and Street environments for testing.

The acoustic scenarios are constructed using clean speech utterances
from the LibriSpeech corpus~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech}.
Specifically, the \texttt{train-clean-360} split is used for training,
the \texttt{dev-clean} split for validation, and the \texttt{test-clean} split for testing.
Each simulated acoustic scenario has a duration of \SI{120.0}{\second}
and features up to $4$ conversational partners.
The speakers randomly start and stop talking, creating highly overlapping speech.
A scenario typically begins with a short period containing only background noise,
lasting between \SI{1.0}{\second} and \SI{5.0}{\second}, before the speakers begin their conversation.
The onset and offset of speech for each conversational partner occur at random intervals,
with the duration between any two consecutive conversational events uniformly sampled
between \SI{1.0}{\second} and \SI{10.0}{\second}. This dynamic
continuously alters the number of concurrently active speakers throughout the scenario.
For these synthetic datasets, the physical sources maintain a minimum distance of \SI{0.5}{\meter}
from each other and the microphone array, and are separated by a minimum direction
of arrival of \SI{30}{\degree} relative to the center of the array.

To investigate generalization capabilities across different hardware configurations,
the four distinct synthetic datasets detailed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets_overview} are constructed.
These datasets are defined along two dimensions:
the array geometry (uniform circular arrays with a fixed radius of \SI{10}{\centi\meter}
versus randomized planar geometries uniformly distributed within a \SI{20}{\centi\meter} $\times$ \SI{20}{\centi\meter} square area)
and the number of microphone channels
(a fixed count of $8$ microphones versus a variable count uniformly sampled between $3$ and $8$ channels).

\subsection{BRUDEX}

The \gls{BRUDEX} database~\cite{fejgin2023brudex} contains \glspl{RIR} and multi-channel noise recordings
captured in various reverberant environments using a dummy head (and external microphones, which are not used in this study).
The dummy head is equipped with both \gls{BTE} hearing aids,
featuring a front and rear microphone on each ear,
as well as in-ear microphones positioned within the ear canals.

Similar to the synthetic dataset, the dynamic acoustic scenarios are constructed
using clean speech utterances from the LibriSpeech corpus~\cite{panayotov2015librispeech},
utilizing the exact same stochastic conversational dynamic
to simulate up to $4$ concurrently active speakers over a duration of \SI{120.0}{\second}.
The physical microphone array configuration is randomly selected for each scenario
from a subset of available array geometries:
all \gls{BTE} and in-ear microphones combined ($6$ channels),
only the \gls{BTE} microphones ($4$ channels),
only the front or only the rear \gls{BTE} microphones ($2$ channels each),
the in-ear microphones combined with either the front or the rear \gls{BTE} microphones ($4$ channels each),
and asymmetric configurations combining the \gls{BTE} microphones of one ear
with the in-ear microphone of the opposite ear ($3$ channels).
This geometric diversity results in varying numbers of input channels
and distinct spatial coordinates.

The data is partitioned into distinct training, validation, and testing sets with non-overlapping spatial and acoustic parameters.
For the spatial distribution of the active sound sources, the directions of arrival are strictly partitioned.
The training set utilizes directions of arrival of
\SI{-30}{\degree}, \SI{60}{\degree}, \SI{-90}{\degree}, \SI{120}{\degree}, \SI{-150}{\degree}, and \SI{180}{\degree}.
The validation set utilizes \SI{30}{\degree}, \SI{-120}{\degree}, and \SI{150}{\degree}.
Finally, the testing set utilizes \SI{0}{\degree}, \SI{-60}{\degree}, and \SI{90}{\degree}.
Furthermore, the environmental noise conditions are strictly separated:
The training set is corrupted by babble and white noise,
the validation set by babble noise,
and the testing set by cafeteria noise.
Across all scenarios, the \gls{SNR} ranges continuously between \SI{5.0}{\decibel} and \SI{20.0}{\decibel},
and the relative source powers are distributed within an interval of \SI{-5.0}{\decibel} to \SI{5.0}{\decibel}.
The acoustic environments are rendered using the \textit{low} reverberation condition
(median reverberation time $RT_{60}\approx \SI{310}{\milli\s}$) provided by the \gls{BRUDEX} database.

The ground truth speech activity in this simulated dataset is defined purely based on acoustic power.
The ground truth speech activity is derived by applying
an energy threshold of \SI{-30}{\decibel} to the clean speech signal components.
Silences shorter than \SI{1.0}{\second} are neglected, ensuring continuous speech activity segments during fluent speech.

\subsection{AMI meeting corpus}

The \gls{AMI} meeting corpus~\cite{mccowan2005ami} contains approximately \SI{100}{\hour} of recorded meetings,
featuring sessions with $3$ to $5$ participants, though at most $4$ speakers are ever simultaneously active.
The recordings were conducted in three distinct instrumented meeting rooms (Edinburgh, Idiap, and TNO),
resulting in heterogeneous distant microphone array configurations across the corpus.
The Edinburgh room features two $8$-channel uniform circular arrays with a radius of \SI{10}{\centi\meter} (Array $1$ and Array $2$).
The Idiap room contains one $8$-channel uniform circular array on the table (Array $1$)
and one $4$-channel uniform circular array mounted on the ceiling (Array $2$).
Finally, the TNO room is equipped with an $8$-channel uniform circular array on the table (Array $1$)
and a $10$-channel linear array (Array $2$).

The data preparation follows the methodology proposed by Cornell et al.~\cite{cornell2022overlapped}.
The ground truth \gls{SAD} labels are derived directly
from the official manual word-level annotations (\texttt{ami\_public\_manual\_1.6.2}),
which were reliably obtained by human annotators utilizing the individual close-talk headset microphones.
The division of the recorded meetings into training, validation, and testing sets strictly adheres
to the official \gls{AMI} Full-corpus-ASR partition, which includes meetings from all three rooms.

During training, a dynamic data augmentation strategy is employed
to expose the model to a well-distributed variety of overlap conditions.
For \SI{50}{\percent} of the training data, unaltered contiguous chunks of \SI{7.0}{\second}
are sampled from random temporal positions within the training meetings.
For the remaining \SI{50}{\percent} of the training data,
dynamically augmented chunks of \SI{7.0}{\second} are generated.
These augmented chunks are constructed by sampling isolated single-speaker chunks
from different meetings and mixing the isolated single-speaker chunks together.
The augmented chunks contain $2$, $3$, or $4$ concurrent speakers,
each with a probability of approximately \SI{33}{\percent}.
To simulate acoustic variations, each additional single-speaker chunk
is mixed with a relative \gls{SIR} shift.
The relative \gls{SIR} shift is drawn from a Gaussian distribution
($\mu = \SI{0}{\decibel}$, $\sigma = \SI{4.0}{\decibel}$) bounded between \SI{-10}{\decibel} and \SI{10}{\decibel}.
To prevent numerical overflow, if the peak amplitude of the augmented chunk exceeds $0.99$,
the augmented chunk is uniformly scaled to a target peak amplitude uniformly drawn
from the interval $[0.5, 0.99]$. 

For validation and testing, contiguous chunks of \SI{7.0}{\second} are extracted sequentially.
A hop length of \SI{5.0}{\second} is used, resulting in a consistent overlap of \SI{2.0}{\second}
between adjacent chunks. This sequential extraction ensures that the validation chunks
and the test chunks are processed in the correct temporal order for continuous evaluation.
